Most visitors ride straight through these stations staring at a phone. Yet you are inside the only museum in the world whose exhibits carry nine million passengers a day and whose entry ticket costs seventy roubles.

Step off at Mayakovskaya, look up, and there are 34 Deineka mosaics in the ceiling domes. Sky, aircraft, parachutists, apple branches. You only see them if you tilt your head back — in 1938 the architect Dushkin placed them there deliberately, so that a commuter rushing for a train would look up anyway.

## Komsomolskaya: baroque underground

We start at the most extravagant station in the city. Shchusev designed it as a throne room: a yellow vault, plasterwork, chandeliers and eight smalt panels of Russian commanders, from Alexander Nevsky to victory on Red Square.

The story of that ceiling is worth hearing on the spot: the panels were reworked twice, and on one of them you can still see where a figure was painted over. Your guide will show you exactly where.

## Novoslobodskaya: 32 stained-glass panels from Riga

Nobody leaves this one quickly. Its pillars hold 32 stained-glass panels made by Latvian craftsmen from glass found in the stores of Riga Cathedral, lit from within and framed in gilded brass.

They look like cathedral windows, except there is no street behind them, only lamps. When a train passes and the air roars, the effect turns genuinely strange: church light and the noise of the metro in a single frame.

## Ploshchad Revolyutsii: 76 bronze figures and a dog with a polished nose

Here the route stops being about architecture and becomes about people. Seventy-six life-size bronzes: a border guard with his dog, a sailor, a student, an engineer, a poultry farmer with a rooster. All of them crouching or bending, because the arches are low and the figures had to fit.

The dogs' noses are polished to gold — students rub them before exams. The tradition started in 1938 and has never stopped. In one minute you will watch five people do it.

## Kiyevskaya and Belorusskaya: mosaics instead of a textbook

Circle-line Kiyevskaya carries 18 gold-ground mosaic panels: scenes from Ukrainian history, weddings, harvests, Bohdan Khmelnytsky. Belorusskaya has twelve ceiling medallions of partisans and weavers, and a floor laid in a pattern copied from Belarusian embroidery.

This is where it becomes clear why any of it was built. The metro of the 1930s and 1950s was conceived as a palace for the people. Families living in barracks descended into marble and bronze every morning. Understanding that changes how the stations feel far more than any architectural note.

## Avtozavodskaya and the finish

We end at a station built in 1943 — mid-war, under bombardment. Its mosaics show the home front: steelworkers, pilots, women at lathes. Materials were scarce, so the station is plainer than the rest, and that only makes the impression stronger.

## How the tour runs

Two hours, groups of up to eight, one fare for the whole route since we never leave the system. Your guide times arrivals so you reach each platform just after a train departs: thirty seconds of relative emptiness is enough to see a station whole.

The route works equally well for first-time visitors and for Muscovites who have ridden these lines for twenty years without once looking up.
