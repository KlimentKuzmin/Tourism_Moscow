There is one moment worth waking up early for. You pass through the Kutafya Tower, cross the bridge over the former moat — and the crowds of Red Square fall away behind you. Ahead lies Cathedral Square, almost empty at ten in the morning. Five white stone churches, gold domes, and a silence you will not find anywhere else in central Moscow. That frame is where this route begins.

## Cathedral Square: where tsars were crowned

The Dormition Cathedral looks restrained from outside, and that is misleading. Inside it lands on you: the vaults rise forty metres, the walls are painted from floor to dome, and hundreds of candles burn in the half-dark. Every Russian ruler from Ivan IV to Nicholas II was crowned here. You are standing on the exact spot where it happened.

The Archangel Cathedral holds 54 tombs. Ivan Kalita, Dmitry Donskoy, Ivan the Terrible and his sons — not an exhibit behind glass but real sarcophagi you can walk right up to. Your guide will tell you which of them died of natural causes and which did not, and after that the sixteenth century stops being a textbook chapter.

The Annunciation Cathedral was the private chapel of the Moscow rulers, the most intimate of the three. Its iconostasis still holds work by Andrei Rublev and Theophanes the Greek — icons more than six hundred years old, hanging where they have always hung.

## The Armoury Chamber: coronation gowns and an ivory throne

Next comes the part our guests remember most. The Armoury is not really a museum; it is a treasury where everything you see was worn, put on for a coronation, or carried into battle.

The Cap of Monomakh. The double throne of Peter I and his brother Ivan V, with the hidden window through which their sister Sophia fed the boys their answers to foreign envoys. Catherine the Great's coronation gown with a 62-centimetre waist. Carriages with windows of real crystal. Ten Fabergé eggs, including the Trans-Siberian Express with a clockwork gold train inside.

The slot lasts ninety minutes, which is exactly enough to walk the halls without rushing. You cannot stay longer — entry is strictly timed.

## Red Square, and a cathedral that is different inside

We leave the Kremlin through the Spassky Gate, the one under the clock tower. And here an effect kicks in that you cannot prepare for: after the quiet of Cathedral Square you step onto Red Square and the scale hits you flat. GUM to the right, the History Museum to the left, St Basil's straight ahead.

From outside, St Basil's reads as one building with nine domes. Inside it is something else entirely: nine separate churches joined by narrow spiral galleries where two people cannot pass. The walls carry floral patterns, the ceilings are low, light falls through small windows. It feels like stepping inside a jewellery box. Almost nobody expects this on a first visit.

## The finish: Zaryadye and the view at sunset

We end two minutes away, on the floating bridge in Zaryadye Park. The bridge breaks off above the Moskva River — water under your feet, the Kremlin walls to your left, the Kotelnicheskaya tower to your right. On the evening departure you arrive exactly at sunset, as the Kremlin stars start to burn red.

It costs nothing, and it is the shot our guests name most often as the best of the trip.
