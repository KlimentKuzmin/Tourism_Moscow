Moscow deserves to be seen from the water at least once. Not because it is pretty, but because from the river the city finally assembles into one thing. From the embankment you get fragments: a bridge here, a dome there. From the deck it lines up into a single continuous shot that drifts past you for two and a half hours.

And if you catch sunset, the reason this route exists kicks in. Over roughly twenty minutes the city switches on: first the floodlights of Christ the Saviour Cathedral, then the Kremlin walls, then Moscow City on the horizon. You stand on the upper deck and watch it happen in real time.

## Departure: Gorky Park and the Krymsky Bridge

We leave from the Gorky Park pier. The first minutes are deceptive — it looks like an ordinary embankment. Then the boat slides under the Krymsky Bridge, Moscow's only suspension bridge, and the view opens onto the Muzeon sculpture park and Tsereteli's monument to Peter the Great: 98 metres of it, argued over by Muscovites for thirty years.

## The Kremlin from the water: an angle you cannot get on foot

Then the main event. The Kremlin Embankment runs alongside the boat for nearly a kilometre. From Red Square you see the Kremlin from inside, from below, in pieces. From the water you get all of it — full length, towers in order: Vodovzvodnaya, Blagoveshchenskaya, Tainitskaya, First and Second Nameless.

This is usually where everyone on deck goes quiet. Then the House on the Embankment slides past to starboard — the grey mass where the Soviet elite lived in the 1930s and out of which the same elite was driven at night. Your guide will tell a couple of stories from that building; they stay with you.

## Red October, Novospassky and the turn

Past the Bolshoy Kamenny Bridge a different Moscow begins: red brick blocks of a former confectionery factory, lofts, rooftop restaurant lights. The river then bends south-east toward Novospassky Monastery, its white walls and bell tower lit warm in the evening.

The boat turns here. And the way back is a completely different trip: the same city, in the dark.

## The return: a city switched on

This is why you take the evening departure. Everything you saw an hour ago in daylight is now burning. Christ the Saviour in gold. The Kremlin in warm yellow, its tower stars in red. The bridges lit from beneath, their reflections shaking in the water.

The finale is Moscow City. The towers rise 370 metres straight out of the water, glass catching the last light, a strip of animation running the full height of the Federation Tower. The contrast with the Kremlin you passed forty minutes earlier is the strongest impression of the whole route. Two cities in one trip.

## Practical notes

The boat is glazed, but the upper deck is open — and that is where you should spend most of the trip. It is noticeably colder on the water than on the embankment, so bring a windbreaker even in July. Winter departures use heated icebreakers, and the winter run has its own force: dark water, ice, and the city lit by four in the afternoon.

We reserve upper-deck seats in advance and pick the departure against the sunset table — the only way to land reliably inside that twenty-minute window.
