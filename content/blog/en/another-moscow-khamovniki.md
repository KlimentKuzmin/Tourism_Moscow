The second visit to Moscow is always harder than the first. You have seen the Kremlin, you have done the Tretyakov, and what comes next is unclear. This route was built for exactly that moment: three hours through a city that lives on former factories, glass and red brick rather than postcards.

Not a single stop here appears on a top-ten list. Which is precisely why people come back from it in better spirits than from the classic centre.

## Khamovniki: constructivism between the tower blocks

We start where the Moscow of the 1920s survives in whole blocks. Communal housing with ribbon windows, round balconies and staircases pushed outside into glass cylinders. In 1928 this was the future: shared kitchens, fourteen-square-metre rooms, the conviction that domestic life should be collectivised.

People still live in these flats, and laundry still hangs in the courtyards. Walking between them is strange and genuinely interesting: a utopia that someone still enters carrying a shopping bag.

Your guide will show you how to tell real constructivism from later imitations, and what became of the architects who built it. That ending is not a happy one.

## The Frunzenskaya Embankment and the crossing

Then we come out to the water. Frunzenskaya is one of the quietest embankments in the centre: lime trees, granite, occasional runners and Stalin-era buildings with anchors on their façades. From here you can see Novodevichy Monastery across the river and the university spires in the distance.

Twenty unhurried minutes along the water is the stretch guests bring up most often afterwards, even though "nothing happens" in it.

## Red October: chocolate, lofts and rooftops

Then the red brick arrives all at once. The former Einem confectionery factory — twenty late-nineteenth-century blocks where chocolate was made until 2007. The smell of cocoa used to hang over the whole of Bolotny Island; older residents say they read the wind direction by it.

Today the workshops hold studios, bars, galleries and newsrooms. The brick was left as it was, the pipes and valves were never removed, the factory windows kept. You could wander here indefinitely: each courtyard leads into the next, until you emerge on the embankment with Christ the Saviour Cathedral directly opposite.

We go up to one of the open rooftops. That is the shot worth doing this route in the evening for.

## GES-2: a power station turned House of Culture

The finale. A 1907 building that supplied current to the Moscow tram for a century, reopened in 2021 after a rebuild by Renzo Piano — the architect of the Centre Pompidou.

Inside: a vast white hall under a glass roof, blue chimneys seventy metres high, a birch grove planted at the entrance, and complete silence. Entry is free. After the brick and the factory lanes, the contrast works physically, as if you had walked into a different city.

The route ends there, on the Bolotnaya Embankment, twenty minutes' walk from Red Square — and by then you will have no desire to go.

## Why this route works

It is honest. We do not pretend Moscow is all gold and domes. We show a city that demolished, rebuilt, went bankrupt and reopened, where the century before last stands flush against glass.

Groups of up to eight, three hours, two coffee stops. It lands equally well with second-time visitors and with Muscovites who have never walked these lanes.

**Duration:** 3 hours. **Price:** 3,900 ₽ per person. **Start:** Frunzenskaya metro station, exit 1.
